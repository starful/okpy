## The Expectations Gap That Drives Early Exits

Most 90-day departures trace back to a mismatch between what a candidate imagined the job would be and what they actually encounter on day one. Standard interviews are built to sell the role, not describe it, so candidates walk in with a polished mental picture that rarely survives contact with a real shift. A realistic job preview (RJP) video inserted into the interview process — showing actual tasks, pace, noise level, customer interactions, and physical demands — closes that gap before an offer is even accepted. Candidates self-select out if the reality doesn't fit, and those who accept arrive with calibrated expectations. Across studies of this intervention, 90-day turnover drops by 10-25% relative to hiring processes that skip the preview.

## Why Video Outperforms Verbal Description

Recruiters and hiring managers routinely describe day-to-day duties verbally, but verbal previews are filtered through the same optimism bias that makes any sales pitch persuasive. Video removes that filter: candidates see unedited or lightly edited footage of the actual floor, warehouse aisle, or call queue, including the less glamorous parts — repetitive tasks, standing for long stretches, or handling a difficult customer call. That unfiltered exposure is what produces the self-selection effect. Candidates who would have struggled with the pace or environment often withdraw voluntarily before an offer, which shifts the entire retention curve rather than requiring managers to identify poor fits after the fact.

## Where the Effect Concentrates

The 10-25% reduction shows up most reliably in roles with high early-tenure attrition to begin with — retail floor associates, call center agents, warehouse pickers, hospitality staff — where the day-to-day reality (shift unpredictability, physical intensity, customer volume) is the primary driver of early quits, not compensation or career trajectory. In roles where turnover stems mostly from pay, scheduling conflicts, or management quality, a preview video won't move the number much, since the video can't fix what candidates already understood accurately going in. The effect also depends on the video actually depicting mundane reality; a preview that reads as another recruitment ad won't produce the expectation-calibration that drives the retention gain.

## Placement in the Hiring Funnel Matters

Timing the video before the final interview, rather than only in an offer letter follow-up, preserves the self-selection benefit — candidates who would withdraw need the chance to do so before sunk cost (accepting an offer, giving notice elsewhere) sets in. Some organizations place the video before scheduling an interview at all, which further reduces recruiter time spent on candidates likely to withdraw once they see the role clearly. The tradeoff is a smaller applicant pool moving forward at each stage, which should be read as a feature of the intervention, not a recruiting shortfall, since the goal is a better-matched pool rather than a larger one.

## Applying It Without Overselling or Underselling

Build the video from footage of real shifts and real employees rather than staged reenactments, and include at least one segment showing a difficult or unglamorous moment alongside the positive aspects of the role. Keep it short enough to watch during a normal interview slot — long enough to convey the pace and environment, not so long that candidates disengage. Track 90-day turnover for cohorts hired with and without the preview to confirm the reduction holds for your specific roles, since the 10-25% range reflects an average across studied contexts and will vary with how accurately your existing interview process already sets expectations.
